**Assignment:** Applied Conflict Style Analysis (1,500 words)
**Submitted:** April 15, 2024
**Audience:** Course instructor and peer reviewers

---

## Summary

For this assignment I analyzed a real conflict from my first campus job, in the
dining hall, between myself and a shift lead over how closing-shift tasks were being
divided. Using the dual-concerns model of conflict styles (competing, avoiding,
accommodating, compromising, collaborating), I traced how my own default style
—avoiding, because I was new and didn't want to seem difficult—actually prolonged the
conflict rather than resolving it.

The paper walks through three stages:

1. **What happened.** A factual account of two closing shifts where task division felt
   unequal, and how each of us communicated (or failed to communicate) about it.
2. **Style diagnosis.** I identified my own avoiding style and my shift lead's
   competing style, and explained — using course concepts on power and relational
   history — why those two styles combined to escalate rather than resolve the issue.
3. **What collaborating would have looked like.** A rewritten version of the
   conversation I should have had in week one, applying active listening and
   interest-based (not position-based) framing.

## Key concept applied

The paper leans on the distinction between **content conflict** (the actual task
division) and **relationship conflict** (what the disagreement implied about respect
and standing on the team) — a distinction I had not had language for before this
course, despite having lived through plenty of both kinds of conflict already.

---

**Reflection** *(added for portfolio)*

This is the first paper in my coursework where I analyzed my own communication
behavior instead of someone else's. It felt uncomfortable to write "I avoided this
conflict and made it worse" in an assignment I was being graded on. But it's also the
first time I understood that communication theory isn't just descriptive — it gives you
a vocabulary for your own patterns, which is the precondition for changing them. I use
that same self-diagnostic habit now when I look at my own drafts in CMST 4714, asking
what intention I actually had versus what a reader would reasonably decode.
