## Summary

Using Roanoke Valley Marketing Group's summer 2024 transition to AI-assisted drafting
tools as my case, I audited how the change was communicated internally — and how
poorly it landed with the copywriting staff, despite the agency's leadership genuinely
trying to do right by their team.

**What the audit found:**

- Leadership announced the new tools in a single all-staff email with a link to a
  vendor training video. There was no channel for writers to ask questions before the
  tools went live on active client accounts.
- The formal message emphasized efficiency ("save hours per week"). The informal
  network — what staff actually said to each other in Slack DMs, which I was able to
  review in aggregate with permission — expressed fear about job security and quality
  control, neither of which leadership had addressed.
- Using Weick's sensemaking framework, I argued that staff were left to construct their
  own explanation for *why* this change was happening, in the absence of one from
  leadership, and the explanation they constructed ("they're testing whether they still
  need us") was more damaging than anything leadership actually intended.

**Recommendation given:** a two-way rollout communication plan — an FAQ built from
actual staff questions, a named point of contact for tool-related concerns, and an
explicit statement from leadership about what would and would not change about
staff roles.

**Reflection** *(added for portfolio)*

Writing this audit about a workplace I had just left, using theory instead of just
venting about it, is what convinced me that organizational communication problems and
"AI is changing communication work" are not two separate topics — they're the same
topic. The gap between formal and informal channels I found here is structurally the
same gap I now analyze in CMST 4714 between what a system is designed to optimize for
and what the people using it actually experience. I didn't expect a sophomore-year
internship to still be useful material two years later.
