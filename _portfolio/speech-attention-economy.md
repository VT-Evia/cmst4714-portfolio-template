## Outline (excerpt)

**Topic:** How social media feeds decide what you see, and why most users don't know it

**Specific purpose:** To inform my audience how algorithmic curation works on the
platforms they use every day, so they can recognize when a feed is making choices for
them.

**I. Introduction**
- Attention-getter: Ask the audience to guess how many posts are available to their
  Instagram account each day versus how many they actually see (average: thousands
  available, roughly 200 shown).
- Thesis: The gap between those two numbers is filled by a recommendation algorithm
  making decisions about what is "relevant" to you — decisions you never agreed to and
  usually never see happen.

**II. Body**
- Main point 1: How ranking algorithms work at a basic level (engagement signals,
  watch time, past behavior) — sourced from public platform transparency reports.
- Main point 2: What gets rewarded by these systems (strong emotional reaction, in-group
  content) versus what a user might say they actually want (balanced information).
- Main point 3: Why this matters for anyone going into a communication-adjacent career —
  if you create content, you are not just writing for a person, you are writing for a
  filter that decides whether a person ever sees it.

**III. Conclusion**
- Restate thesis
- Call to action: audit your own feed for one week and notice what kind of content it
  is quietly promoting to you

**Reflection** *(added for portfolio)*

I wrote this speech because a recommendation algorithm had frustrated me personally
that semester, not because I had any theoretical framework for it — I didn't yet know
the words "encoding," "decoding," or *intentio algorithmi*. Rereading the outline now,
as a senior finishing CMST 4714, I can see the instinct that eventually became the
argument at the center of my capstone work in that course: that a system standing
between a writer and a reader is not a neutral pipe, it has its own goals. I didn't have
the vocabulary for that at eighteen. I just had the annoyance. It's satisfying to see
where that annoyance eventually led.
